Methodological Overview

Infectious disease phylodynamics seeks to infer pathogen transmission patterns, demographic history, and spatial dissemination directly from viral or bacterial genome sequences. While genomic data provide high temporal resolution on ancestral branching, testing hypotheses about why pathogens disperse through specific geographical corridors requires integrating environmental, climatic, and topological covariates.

Traditionally, integrating Earth observation rasters into phylodynamic workflows involves cumbersome local GIS pipelines: downloading hundreds of gigabytes of satellite tiles, clipping to study boundaries, re-projecting, aggregating across time windows, and manually formatting pairwise matrix structures. PhyloCov eliminates this friction by leveraging Google Earth Engine's distributed planetary computational infrastructure, exposing two specialized pipelines:

1. Continuous Phylodynamics (Seraphim)

Cloud export of high-resolution, georeferenced GeoTIFF raster layers for Continuous Relaxed Random Walk (RRW) diffusion models. Used in the R package seraphim to test whether environmental surfaces act as conductances or resistances to pathogen movement across continuous space.

2. Discrete Phylodynamics (BEAST GLM)

Concurrent extraction of environmental covariates for discrete sample coordinates or boundary polygons (GeoJSON/Shapefiles), producing fully formatted, standardized $K \times K$ predictor matrices ready for direct import into BEAST / BEAUti discrete diffusion models.

Mathematical Formulations of GLM Predictors

In discrete phylogeography, transition rates between $K$ distinct geographic demes are modeled as a continuous-time Markov chain (CTMC). Following Lemey et al. (2014), the Generalized Linear Model (GLM) parameterizes the transition rate $\Lambda_{ij}$ between origin deme $i$ and destination deme $j$ as a log-linear function of $P$ potential predictors:

log(Λij) = β0 + ∑k=1...P βk × δk × Xk, ij

where:

  • β0 is the baseline transition rate intercept.
  • βk represents the effect size coefficient (slope) quantifying the contribution of predictor $k$. A positive $\beta_k$ indicates an increase in lineage transition rate with higher predictor values.
  • δk ∈ {0, 1} is a binary indicator variable operating under a spike-and-slab mixture prior, used to evaluate predictor inclusion probability and calculate Bayes factors.
  • Xk, ij is the value of the $k$-th pairwise predictor between location $i$ and location $j$.

1. Geographic Distance Predictors

Geographic proximity is the foundational driver of pathogen dispersal (gravity model). PhyloCov calculates great-circle distance $d_{ij}$ in kilometers using the Haversine formula between location centroids:

a = sin²(Δφ / 2) + cos(φi) × cos(φj) × sin²(Δλ / 2)
dij = 2 × R × arctan2(√a, √(1 - a))   [where R = 6371.0 km]

Because spatial dispersal resistance typically scales non-linearly with distance, PhyloCov generates both raw distance ($d_{ij}$ in km) and log-transformed distance ($\log d_{ij}$). Standardizing log-distance provides the canonical baseline against which ecological covariates are evaluated.

2. Environmental Covariate Matrices

For each environmental layer $C$ extracted from Earth Engine (e.g. precipitation, temperature, elevation, NDVI), PhyloCov generates 4 pairwise directional hypotheses testing distinct transmission mechanisms:

Matrix Type Formulation Biological Hypothesis Tested
Origin Xij = Ci Emigration driver: Tests whether higher environmental values at the source increase outbound transmission (e.g. higher temperatures driving vector density and outbound viral export).
Destination Xij = Cj Immigration driver: Tests whether higher values at the destination facilitate successful lineage establishment and persistence.
Absolute Difference Xij = |Ci - Cj| Ecological barrier: Tests whether differences in ecology act as barriers to movement (isolation-by-environment), where larger differences reduce transmission.
Pairwise Average Xij = (Ci + Cj) / 2 Corridor suitability: Tests whether lineage movement is higher when both locations share favorable environmental conditions.

3. Off-Diagonal Z-Score Standardization

In CTMC substitution models, self-transitions (diagonal entries $X_{ii}$) are non-events and must equal 0.0. To ensure Bayesian priors (e.g., standard Normal or Laplace shrinkage priors) behave properly and effect sizes $\beta_k$ across heterogeneous physical units (e.g., mm/day vs °C vs meters) are directly comparable, PhyloCov standardizes matrices across their off-diagonal elements:

μoff = Σi ≠ j Xij / (K(K - 1))
σoff = √[ Σi ≠ j (Xij - μoff)² / (K(K - 1) - 1) ]
X̃ij = (Xij - μoff) / σoff   [for i ≠ j;  X̃ii = 0.0]

Standardization Best Practice

PhyloCov generates both raw matrices (e.g. matrix_chirps_origin.csv) and standardized matrices (e.g. matrix_chirps_origin_std.csv). Always import the standardized (*_std.csv) matrices into BEAUti. Unstandardized matrices with widely differing variances will distort MCMC acceptance rates and bias Bayes factors.

Step-by-Step Guide: BEAST / BEAUti GLM Setup

Below is the step-by-step procedure for incorporating PhyloCov's generated GLM predictor package into BEAUti (BEAST v1.10.x / v1.11.x):

1

Extract Predictors in PhyloCov

Upload your sample spreadsheet or boundary shapefile in PhyloCov. Select your desired environmental layers, enable "BEAST GLM Predictor Matrices", and download the resulting .zip package.

2

Configure Discrete Trait in BEAUti

Open BEAUti. Navigate to the Traits panel and create a new discrete trait (e.g. location). Map your taxa IDs to their discrete state names matching the locations in location_summary.csv.

3

Enable GLM Diffusion Model

Under the Sites panel for your discrete trait, select the Generalized Linear Model (GLM) diffusion extension. In the GLM settings, specify whether the transition matrix is symmetric or asymmetric.

4

Import Standardized Predictor Matrices

In the GLM Predictors panel, click "Import Predictor". Import matrix_great_circle_distance_log_std.csv as your baseline spatial predictor. Next, import any environmental hypotheses of interest (e.g., matrix_era5_origin_std.csv, matrix_chirps_abs_difference_std.csv).

5

Set Prior Distributions

Under the Priors panel, ensure the GLM coefficients $\beta_k$ have appropriate shrinkage priors (e.g., Normal($0, \sigma^2=2.0$)). The inclusion indicator $\delta_k$ typically has a Bernoulli prior with expected inclusion equal to $\frac{\log 2}{P}$ or $50\%$ depending on hypothesis sparsity.

6

Evaluate Bayes Factors in Tracer

Run your MCMC chain in BEAST. In Tracer, examine the marginal posterior probability of inclusion for each predictor $k$ ($p_k = P(\delta_k = 1 | \text{data})$). Compute Bayes factors relative to the prior probability $p_0$:

BFk = [ pk / (1 - pk) ] / [ p0 / (1 - p0) ]

Conventionally, $BF > 3$ indicates positive support, $BF > 10$ indicates strong support, and $BF > 100$ indicates decisive statistical evidence.

Continuous Phylogeography & Landscape Genetics (Seraphim)

For pathogens exhibiting continuous spatial expansion (e.g., rabies in wildlife, West Nile virus in birds, seasonal influenza), lineages diffuse across continuous geographic coordinates rather than jumping between discrete demes.

PhyloCov's Pipeline 1 (Continuous Phylodynamics) exports high-resolution, georeferenced GeoTIFF environmental rasters directly from Earth Engine. In the R package seraphim (Dellicour et al. 2016), these rasters are analyzed against posterior tree distributions generated from Continuous Relaxed Random Walk (RRW) models:

1

Continuous Tree Inference in BEAST

Sample viral sequences with continuous lat/lon coordinates. In BEAST, fit a Continuous Relaxed Random Walk (RRW) with a Cauchy or log-normal distribution on branch diffusion rates.

2

Export Study Extent GeoTIFFs from PhyloCov

In PhyloCov Pipeline 1, select your region of interest (e.g. South Africa, Kenya, or custom bounding box). Choose your environmental variables (e.g. SRTM elevation, MODIS NDVI, ERA5 temperature), adjust the temporal window, and export the GeoTIFF.

3

Landscape Path Extraction in Seraphim

In R, use seraphim::extractPath() to trace individual phylogenetic branches over the environmental raster. Test whether movement duration correlates with environmental conductance or resistance using least-cost path or circuit theory algorithms (via gdistance).
